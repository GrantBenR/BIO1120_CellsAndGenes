- Prokaryotic cells divide through a simple form of division called binary fission
- Results in a pair of daughter cells that are genetically identical to the parent cell
- Mitosis is unnecessary because there is no nucleus or multiple chromosomes

### Binary fission of a bacterium

- Chromosome and cytoplasmic separation
    - FtsZ forms a ring around the periphery of the midpoint between the chromosomes
- Septum formation
    - this divides the cell
- Cell division
    - The cell pinches in two forming two daughter cells

### Eukaryotic chromosomes
- Genomic DNA
- Genome: all the genetic information inside a cell
    - In prokaryotes: the genome is composed of a single circular double-stranded DNA molecules
    - In eukaryotes: the genome is in several double-stranded, linear DNA molecules bound with proteins to form complexes called chromosomes
        - Human somatic cells have 46 chromosomes (23 identical pairs -> diploid)
        - Human sex cells contain one set of 23 chromosomes (haploid) called gametes.

### Chromosomes

### Eukaryotic cell reproduction
- An integral part of the cell cycle

### The cell cycle
- An ordered series of events involving cell growth and cell division that produces two new daughter cells
- most cells in G1
- Phases
    - Interphase
        - G1
            - majority of cell growth and protein synthesis
        - S - DNA Synthesis
        - G2 - Cell Growth 2
    - Mitosis
    - Cytokinesis

### Mitosis
- Prophase
    - Chromosomes condense and become visible
    - Spindle fibers emerge from the centrosomes
    - Nuclear envelope breaks down
    - Centrosomes move toward opposite poles
- Prometaphase
    - Chromosomes continue ot condense
    - Kinetochores appear at the centromeres
    - Mitotic spindle microtubules attach to kinetochores
- Metaphase
    - Chromosomes are lined up at the metaphase plate
    - Each sister chromatid is attached to a spindle fiber originating from opposite poles
- Anaphase
    - Centromeres split in two
    - Sister chromatids (now called chromosomes) are plled toward opposite poles
    - Centain spindle fibers begin to elongate the cell
- Telophase
    - Chromosomes arrive at opposite poles and begin to decondense
    - Nuclear envelope material surrounds each set of chromosomes
    - The mitotic spindle breaks down
    - Spindle fibers continue to push poles apart
- Cytokinesis
    - Animal cells: a cleavage furrow separates the daughter cells
    - Plant cells: a cell plate, the precursor to a new cell wall separates the daughter cells

### Mendel's conclusions
- When Mendel crossed violet flowers with white, all the offspring were violet (F1 generation)

- Mendel concluded that characteristics could be divided into expressed and latent traits (dominant and recessive)
    - Dominant traits are those that are inherited unchanged in a hybridization
    - Recessive traits become latent, or invisible (but unchaged) in the offspring of a hybridization

### Law of dominance
- For each characteristic an organism inherits two alleles, one from each parent
- In a heterozygote, one trait will conceal presence of another trait for the same characteristic - law of dominance
- The dominant allele determines the organism's appearnace

### Law of segregation
- The two alleles for a heritable character seperate during gamete formation and end up in different gametes
- This means that the offspring have an equal likelihood of inheriting either factor.
- Mendel's segregation model accounts for the 3:1 ratio observed in the F2 generation of his numerous crosses
- Punnet Squares can be used to determine the ways traits can segregate

### Punnett Square
- Helps predict possible genotypes and pheontypes of the offspring
- His book, Mendelism, was the first textbook on genetics (1905)
- He got G.H. Hardy to explain why a dominant allele did not become fixed in a population

- Helps predict possible genotypes and phenotypes of the offspring

### Test Cross
- The dominant-expressing organism is crossed with an organism that is homozygous recessive for the same characteristic
- The phenotype of the offspring can be used to determine if the organism expressing a dominant trait is a homozygote or a heterozygote

### Law of independent assortment
- Genes do not influence each other with regard to sorting of alleles into gametes
- Every possible combination of alleles is equally likely to occur
- Illustrated by a dihybrid cross

### Connection to Meiosis
- In Meiosis I, different homologous pairs line up in random orientations
- Each gamete can contain any combination of paternal and maternal chromosomes
- Leads to a variety of possible genetic arrangements

### Pedigree Analyses
- Humans are not good organisms for genetic modeling
- Human genetics usually need to rely on analyses of inheritance in naturally occurring families
- In pedigrees, circles represent females, squares males, and filled symbols afflicated individuals.

### Hardy-Weinberg principle
- Mathematically explains genetic equilibrium
- Begins with a binomial expansion of the equation: `p + q = 1`
- Hardy-Weinberg Equation: `p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1`
- p^2 = Frequnecy Homozygous Dominant (AA)
- 2pq = Frequency Heterozygous (Aa)
- q^2 = Frequency Homozygous Recessive (aa)

### Non-Mendelian Inheritance
- Incomplete dominance
    - Neither allele is dominant
    - Heterozygous individuals have an intermediate phenotype
    - Pink snapdragon flowers - have one red alllele and one white allele
- Codominance
    - Both alleles for the same characteristic are simultaneously expressed in the heterozygote
    - Example ABO blood groups of humans.
- Multiple alleles
    - Multiple alleles may exist at the population level, such that many combinations of two alleles are observed
    - The most common phenotype or genotype in the natural population is denoted as the wild type
    - All other phenotypes or genotypes are considered variants to this type
    - Example: ABO blood type
- Sex-linked traits
    - traits (genes) located on the sex chromosomes
    - Sex chromosomes are X and Y
        - XX genotype for females
        - XY genotype for males
    - Many sex-linked recessive disorders are therefore more common in males than in females.
    - For a recessive X-linked trait to be expressed:
        - A female needs two copies of the allele (homozygous)
        - A male needs only one copy of the allele (hemizygous)
    - X-linked recessive disorders are therefore more commmon in males than in females.
- Pleiotropy
    - Single genes often have multiple effects
    - For instance, albino individuals are also often cross eyed
    - The gene that causes albinism can also cause defects in the nerve connections between eyes and the brain.
- Epistasis
    - An interaction between genes such that one gene masks or interferes with the expression of another
    - The alleles that are being masked or silenced are said to be hypostatic
    - Example: Coat color in horses
- Polygenic inheritance
    - When inheritance for a characteristic depends on the combined effects of numerous genes
    - Example: skin color in humans (at least 3 genes)
- Cytoplasmic inheritance
    - Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own chromosomes
    - Example: variegated leaves due to random sampling of organelles during mitotic cell division
    - mitochondria are passed down maternally exclusively
- Mosaicism
    - Female mammals inactivate one of their two X chromosomes (Barr bodies) early in embryonic development
    - Example: calico cats are usually females with X chromosomes that code for two different coat colors.

### DNA Replication
- Watson and Crick
- "It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material.
- Nature, April 25, 1953: "Molecular Structure of Nucleic Acids: A structure for deoxyribose nucleic acid.

- Each strand serves as a template from which the new complementary strand is copied
- New strands are copied from the parental strand.
- Each new double strand will have one parental strand and one new daughter strand.

- Watson and Crick's model suggested that DNA was semi-conservative
- Alternatives:
    - semi-conservative: Pairs of new and old
    - Conservative: One fully old pair, one fully new pair
    - Dispersive: Every strand is part-new and part-old

### Mismatch repair

### The Central Dogma
