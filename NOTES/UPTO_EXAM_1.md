### Learning Objectives

### Basics
- Elements are the building blocks of all matter
- Matter is made up of atoms
- Atoms are the smallest part of an element and are composed of 3 main particles: Protons (+), Neutrons, and Electrons (-)

### Neutrons
- Subatomic particles that weigh 1.7 * 10^-24 g
- Have no charge
- Reside in atomic nuclei
### Protons
- Subatomic particles with same weight as neutron
- Reside in atomic nuclei
- Have positive charge
### Electrons
- Subatomic particles that weigh 1/2000 of the weight of other subatomic particles
- Orbit the atomic nuclei

### Atoms
- Are mostly just empty space
- Area between nucleus and orbiting electrons is often relatively large

### Elements
- Only 25 elements are essential for human life
- While there are 25 necessary, 96% of our bodies come from just 4: oxygen, carbon, nitrogen, and hydrogen
- The last 4% is from just four more elements: Phosphorus, Sulfur, Calcium, and Potassium
- Have one proton for each electron

- An element's atomic number tells us how many protons are in its atoms
- An element's mass number tells us how many protons and neutrons are in its atoms
- Atoms that have the same atomic number but different mass number are called <b>isotopes</b>
- Isotopes that have unstable nuclei which break down over time are called <b>radioactive isotopes</b>

### Hydrogen
- Has one proton and one electron
- Atomic Number: 1

### Valence
- Electrons determine the chemical behavior of an atom
- Electrons exist at energy levels that form shells around the nucleus
    - The closest shell holds up to 2 electrons
    - The second and third shell holds up to 8 electrons
- An atom is at its most stable when all of the electron positions in the outermost shell (<b>valence shell</b>) are filled.
- The number of unpaired electrons in the outermost orbital determines the properties/behaviour of an element
- This is called the <b>valence</b> of the atom 
- The Octet Rule - Atoms want to have a full valence shell of 8

### Chemical Bonds
- The chemical behavior of an atom depends on the number of electrons in its valence 
    - 1_H: 1s orbital half-full, one bond
    - 6_C: 4 Level 2 orbitals half-full, four bonds
    - 7_N: 3 level 2 orbitals half-full, three bonds
    - 8_O: 2 level 2 orbitals half-full, two bonds
- Atoms will combine to form compounds in order to reach eight electrons in their outer energy level (Octet Rule)
### Covalent bonds
- The bonds form when an electron is shared between two elements
    - <b>Nonpolar covalent bonds</b> form between two atoms of the same element or between different elements that share the electrons equally.
    - In <b>polar covalent bonds</b>, the electrons are shared unequally by the atoms.
### Hydrogen Bonds
- Interaction involving a hydrogen atom located between a pair of other atoms having a high affinity for electrons
- Example: bonds between multiple H20 molecules
### Ionic Bonds
- The bond between oppositely charged ions in a chemical compound
- Example: the positive (Na+) and negatice (cl-) charges attract. These ions stay together and form an ionic bond
- Water does a good job of dissolving salt since its polar charges pull apart the strong positive and negative charges in an ionic bond
### Van der Waals Interactions
- Molecules that are afraid of water sometimes form weak bonds with others.

# 9_8 (Mon) - Lipids

### Learning Objectives
- Describe the basic structural characteristics and biological importance of carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids.
- Lipids are hydrophobic fats

- Macromolecules
- Lipids
    - Saturated fats
    - Unsaturated fats
    - Phospholipids

### 4 Major Classes of Biomolecules
- Carbohydrates
- Lipids
- Proteins
- Nucleic Acids

- Carbon is a central element to life because all biological macromolecules are built on a carbon framework.

### Carbohydrates
- Made from C, H, O
- Represented by (CH20)n where n is the number of C atoms in the molecule
- Polymer monomers: saccharides
- Polymer bond name: glycosidic bond
- 3 commonly encountered subtypes:
    - Monosaccharides
    - Dissaccharides

### Lipids
- Hydrophobic
- Are not true polymers
- Polar dissolves polar
- Non-polar dissolves non-polar
- Consist mostly of hydrocarbon regions
- Represented by (CH2)n where n is the number of C atoms in the molecule
- Polymer monomers: fatty acids and glycerol
- Polymer bond name: ester bonds
- Cells store energy for long-term use in the form of lipids called fats
- Fat molecule: Consists of two main components: glycerol and fatty acids.

### Fats
- Constructed from two types of smaller molecules: glycerol and fatty acids
- Glycerol is a three-carbon alcohol with a hydroxyl group attached to each carbon
- A fatty acid consists of a carboxyl group attached to a long carbon skeleton
- In a fat, three fatty acids are joined to a glycerolby an ester linkage, creating a Triacylglycerol, or triglyceride. The fatty acids in a fat can be all the same or of two or three different kinds.

### Isomers
- Compounds with the same molecular formula but different structures and properties
    - Structural isomers have different covalent arrangements of their atoms
    - Cis-trans isomers (also called geometric isomers) have the same covalent bonds but differ in their spatial arrangements.
    - Enantiomers are isomers that are mirror images of each other

### Enantiomers
- Important in the pharmaceutical industry
- Two enantiomers of a drug may have different effects
- Often only one enantiomer is biologically active
- Differering effects of enenantiomers demonstrate that organisms are sensitive to even subtle variations in molecules.

### Fatty Acids
- Vary in length (number of carbons) and in the number and locations of double bonds
- Saturated Fatty Acids: have the maximum number of hydrogen atoms possible and no double bonds
    - tend to be solid at room temperatures
- Unsaturated fatty acids: have one or more oduble bonds
    - Tend to be liquid at room temperature

### Trans fats
- Hydrogenation is the process of converting unsaturated fats to saturated fats by adding hydrogen
- Hydrogenating vegetable oils also creates unsaturated fats with trans double bonds
- These trans fats may contribute more than saturated fats to cardiovascular disease

### Purpose of fats
- The major function of fats in energy storage
- Humans and other mammals store their long-term food reserves in adipose cells
- Adipose tissue also cushions vital organs and insulates the body.

### Phospholipids
- Amphipathic (hydrophobic and hydrophillic)
- Key ocmponent of all cell membranes consists of three main components - glycerol, two fatty acids, and a phosphate group
- Form lipid bilayer sphere in water, hydrophillic end facing out, hydrophobic ends facing in

### Steriods
- Are lipids characterized by a carbon skeleton consisting of four fused rings
### Cholesterol
- A type of steroid, is a component in an animal cell membranes and a precursor from which other steroids are synthesized
- A high level are found in sex hormones: estradiol, testosterone
### Summary
- Monosaccharides serve as a major fuel for cells and raw building molecules
- Polysaccharides, polymerized form of monosaccharides, play storage and structural roles
- Lipids are a diverse group of hydrophobic molecules
    - Major function of fats is energy storage

# 9_10 (Wed) - Proteins

### Key Points
- Macromolecules
- Proteins
    - amino acids
    - peptide bonds
    - polypeptides
    - 1deg, 2deg, 3deg, and 4deg structure
### 4 Classes of Biomolecules (Review)
- Carbohydrates
- Lipids
- Proteins
- Nucleic Acids

### Proteins
- Contain C, H, O plus Nitrogen
- Formed from amino acid monomers joined together
    - 20 different chemically distinct amino acids that form long chains and the amino acids can be in any order
    - The sequence and number of amino acids determine a protein's shape, size, and function.
- Functions:
    - Aids chemical activities in the body: Enzymes , hormones
    - Provides structure: Collagen
    - Transports substances into or out of cells
- Polymer monomers: amino acids
- Polymer bond name: covalent peptide bond (dehydration reaction)
- 3 well known examples:
    - insulin (51 amino acids long; hormone)
    - Hemoglobin (a tetramer)

- Account for more than 50% of the dry mass of most cells
- Proteins speed up chemical reactions
    - Enzymes are proteins that act as catalysts that speed up chemical reactions
    - Enzymes can perform their functions repeatedly functioning as workhorses that carry out the processes of life
    - Other protein functions include defense, storage, transport, cellular communication, movement, and structural support.
- Proteins are all constructed from the same set of 20 amino acids
### Polypeptides
- unbranched polymers built from these amino acids. The bond between amino acids is a peptide bond (dehydration reaction)

- A protein is a biologically functional molecule that consists of one or more polypeptides

### Amino acids form Polypeptides. Polypeptides form Proteins

### Amino Acids
- All amino acids have: an amino group (-NH2), a carboxyl group (-COOH), a central (alpha) carbon, a hydrogen, and a variable "R" group

- A polypeptide is a polymer of amino acids
- Polypeptides range in length from a few to more than 1000 monomers
- Each polypeptide has a unique linear sequence of amino acids, with a carboxyl end (C-terminus) and an amino end (N-terminus)

### Shape Dictates Function in Proteins
- The specific activities of proteins result from their intricate three-dimensional architecture
- A functional protein consists of one or more polypeptides precisely twisted, folded, and coiled into a unique shape
- A protein's strucuture determines how it works
    - The function of a protein usually depends on its ability to recognize and bind to some other molecule
    - The sequence of amino acids determins a protein's three-dimensional structure.

### Levels of protein organization
- Primary protein structure is a sequence of a chain of amino acids
    - like the order of letters in a long word
    - Determined by inherited genetic information
- Secondary protein structure occurs when the sequence of amino acids are linked by hydrogen bonds
    - Coils and folds result from hydrogen bonds between the repeating constituents of the polypeptide backbone
    - Typical structures are a coil called an alpha helix and a folded structure called a beta pleated sheet
- Tertiary protein structure
    - The overall shape of a polypeptide, results from interactions between R groups, rather than interactions between backbone constituents
    - These interactions include hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and van der Waals interactions
    - Strong covalent bonds called disulfide bridges may reinforce the proteins strucuture
- Quanternary Protein Structure
    - results when two or more polypeptide chains form one macromolecule
    - Colagen is a fibrous protein consisting of three polypeptides coiled like a rope
    - Hemoglobin is a globular protein consisting of four polypeptides: two alpha and two beta subunits

### Sickle Cell Disease
- A slight change in primary structure can affect a protein's structure and ability to function
- SCD is an inherited blood disorder, results from a single amino acid substitution in the protein hemoglobin
- The abnormal hemoglobin molecules cause the red blood cells to aggregate into chains and to deform into a sickle shape

### Denaturation
- The loss of a protein's native structure
    - A denatured protein is biologically inactive
    - Extremely high fevers can be fatal: proteins in the blood tend to denature at very high body temperatures

### Bioinformatics
- an approach to prediction of protein structure from amino acid sequences
- Scientists use X-Ray crystallography to determine a protein's structure
- Another mehtod is Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR)
### Flow of genetic info: DNA -> RNA -> Proteins

### Nitrogenous bases
- Purines (2 rings)

### Double Stranded DNA
- Contains hydrogen bonds between purines and pyrimidines
    - T-A
    - G-C
- These bonded pairs are connected via phosphodiester bonds between 5' carbon and 3'carbon 

### Base Pairing
- 5'-GGATGCATG-3'
- 3'-CCTACGTAC-5'
- Nucleic acids are only extended at their 3' ends
- The two strands in double stranded DNA are anti-parallel
- The two strands in double stranded DNA are also reverse complements of each other.

### tRNA (transfer RNA)
- typically single stranded but can base pair
- T arm
- D arm
- Acceptor Stem
